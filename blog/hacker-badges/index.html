2,500 PCB Hacker Badges at Hack The North 2026

Taking a hardware badge from schematic to volume production, and everything that went wrong on the way

On the Saturday of Hack the North, Canada’s biggest hackathon, a team walked up to me holding 4 of our badges. They’d built Among Us on them. Mesh networked together with no central server.

Eight months earlier, those badges were going to be made of cardstock.

Why not cardstock

In mid January, my friend Alex asked if I wanted to join Hack the North. The second I got that message, an idea popped into my head: what if we brought the badgelife culture of cybersecurity events into the world of hackathons. What if we could make a wave so big other hackathon organizers couldn’t ignore it. What if we brought hardware badges into the mainstream of hackathons.

I quickly put together a deck, and pitched the idea to the organizing team.

Here’s the summary:

  • LEDs (as many as we can fit)
  • Screen
  • Battery
  • Buttons
  • Audio
  • Wireless features
  • Cool shape
  • Silkscreen art
  • Lanyards

Oh, and all for $5 / head (boy was I naive).

I made it super clear that we’d need multiple hardware iterations to de-risk and guarantee the success of this project. We initially scoped for an EVT, DVT, and 2-3 PVTs. We wound up shipping in four iterations, with the fourth being our production run.

One of the strong points I had going was that we could rapidly de-scope to blank badges. Without this, I don’t think this project would have taken off. The directors, very understandably, did not want this to (perhaps literally) blow up in our faces. (Foreshadowing.)

The initial kickoff meeting ran quite late. As you can see we had quite a few messages in the thread too ranging from concerns to really cool ideas!

Engineering Validation Test (EVT)

I had so much energy going into the initial schematic. It’s like the initial development of a software side projects. Just pure forward development with no scaling problems, bugs, or debt.

Here it is! In all its beautiful simplicity. There turned out to be quite a few issues, but we of course only found out after ordering!

As for layout, well, I kinda took charge on it and of course went for the timeless classic: the hexagon. Why? Because HEXAGON BESTAGON.

The team were not hexagon ball knowers unfortunately. (RIP hexagon)

We ordered the hexagon just as an engineering test, knowing we’d change it in the future.

There were unfortunately more serious issues than shape though:

  1. Part selection error

Can you spot the error? Hint it’s in the line ordering C10, C14, L1. Wait… how can L1 be the same part as… oh. I labelled C10 and C14 as 10u, and unfortunately L1 as 10u as well. This tricked the part finder into giving me a 10uF cap in the place of a 10uH inductor. The opposite of what I wanted!

Lesson

Don’t blindly trust the part finder. Check twice.

  1. Pins too dense to solder

The initial plan for attaching the screen to the PCB was to use some adhesive on the back of the screen, and directly solder the FPC cable to the board. This was a pretty bad idea. It’s not manufacturable, and it makes the screens nearly un-replaceable without hot air.

The experienced industrial design engineers are groaning, I can practically hear them. We were trying to save a few cents on BOM as budget was tight. This was not the place to do it. We wound up getting away with simply slapping an FPC connector straight onto the existing pads. This was very lucky.

Lesson

Don’t ship manufacturing problems off to the manufacturer. Own them, figured them out, and be clear with them.

Then, a few days later…

The badge is real!

We hopped right onto a call to chat about it.

Debugging it with some test firmware. Keep in mind this was before the days of zero shotting this sort of thing.

But did it work? Yes! (mostly)

All features on the badge worked except for NFC. Well, it did work, it just did entirely the wrong thing. We wanted the NFC chip to scan tags. This NFC chip was a standard chip you’d find in an sticker or card. WHAT! Yep, I should have done my research on how NFC works. Turns out the chip we wanted would be too expensive. We wanted full duplex NFC.

Why duplex NFC? We wanted to be able to bring two badges together and share contact details over NFC! This was also a terrible idea. Wireless exists for a reason. NFC is far too finnicky especially for an unoptimized and barely tuned antenna.

Lesson

Double check the chip does what you actually need, before focusing on how to integrate it. The bias of the right pins being there for what we wanted blinding me to the fact that it didn’t do what we needed.

Design Validation Test (DVT)

Our DVT was more accurately described as an EVT version two. Nonetheless, we had a Linear tracker to keep happy!

Now, the first priority was to fix the NFC issue. But if duplex is too expensive, …

Here was the finalized schematic:

On to design! I had the pleasure of working with two very talented designers. This was fortuitus as my PCB art skills are… brutalist.

The first thing we ironed out with the design team was shape. We knew we wanted something that held like a portable game console. Not unlike one released in 1989 that I may have made an emulator for at one point.

Then came the incredible art. The design team went all out with color silkscreen printing!

Now, the astute reader will notice we had single color silkscreen badges in the end.

JLC offers a color silkscreen service, but there’s a catch. You have to use EasyEDA. Up until this point, we had been using KiCAD. No problem, we’ll just import into EasyEDA and then add our silkscreen layers there! This unfortunately did not work out for us. Something about our ground plane flood was breaking EasyEDA and we did not have the time to fix the issue. We had to de-scope.

Lesson

Be ready and have a plan to de-scope. You have to ruthlessly de-scope to get a badge project across the finish line. This was an excellent call made in partnership with Alex.

As with the EVT, a few weeks later the DVT arrived! Boy were we excited. I had the flightradar24 on all day at work watching the plane holding our badges fly across the Pacific Ocean. (We were in SF.)

In the meantime, we were mesmerized by all the cool things that happen when you order a PCB, like seeing the x-ray inspection:

And then, they finally came.

I shipped them to my office, and I stayed basically the whole night playing with them. I vibecoded an entire OS and basic apps to test in roughly an hour. We realized very quickly that this was a “perfect” DVT. All features worked.

This was the app menu after another few minutes and million odd tokens. The colors were all off. Turns out I actually had two nibbles flipped. We only realized a few weeks later and were vibecoding on wonky screens thinking this was just the quality of the screens. facepalm

As we started working on the next hardware cycle, the firmware side came into fruition. We very quickly realized the power of this thing. We vibecoded a Lua stack that the hackers could use to make their very own apps!

I think this was the moment we realized we were sitting on something big. If executed well, this could be huge. This was something that just had to happen.

Production Validation Test (PVT)

With the incredible momentum and high of a “perfect” DVT, we felt ready to tackle our full scale production test.

Making 5 badges was one thing, but doing it at scale turned out to be a whole other beast. As a somewhat experienced software engineer, I saw some challenges coming, but not in the spades, hearts, diamonds, and clubs they came in. Buckle up for some lesson admonitions.

We ordered 50 for this round. New design + a few moved components. Nothing too major. Here’s where it all went wrong:

  1. Secret feature: smoke!

Our badges were working with the batteries put in the right way. But of course, we cannot expect the user to do put them in the right way!

Lesson

When building hardware, you cannot trust any form of user interaction, especially if it’s safety critical. Better yet, test every form of user interaction. This should have been obvious, but I think my subconscious was putting it off as “out of scope” and almost didn’t want to think about it.

  1. The flipped ICs

Allow me to set the scene: I was in SF, while most of the team was in Waterloo. We decided to send our PVT run straight to Waterloo in the hopes everything would just work and we could pass around the badges to members of the team to begin testing. Wishful thinking.

The badges arrived in Waterloo.

Lesson

Be ready for rework. Don’t be afraid of rework.

  1. NorthSec

NorthSec is Canada’s premier security conference. Almost like a Canadian DEFCON. They’ve been running hardware badges for many years. I attended their CTF along with my awesome team. Their badge was fantastic, featuring dazzling reverse mounted LEDs, SAO headers, buttons, a PCIe connector, etc. The badge was designed with a screen in mind, however as I understand it due to supplier limitations they were not able to get the screens on time.

In their closing ceremonies they went into all the challenges they faced with their badge. They could have practically been reading our Slack. Every badge-killer point they covered almost described us verbatim. By now I was getting quite stressed.

The PVT was supposed to be perfect and we were supposed to simply click “re-order” with a different quantity. This was no longer possible due to problems #1 and #2, and we were still short on a few pre-ordered parts.

Lesson

Learn from your peers. There is an incredible community of badge developers. https://badge.life/

Picking up the pieces

You just begin. You do the math, you solve one problem. Then you solve the next one, and then the next and if you solve enough problems you get to come home.

  • Mark Watney

If you haven’t seen or read The Martian then frankly stop reading this and go read/watch it.

While our project was not as critical as getting the first ever space pirate home, I’d like to think that just about any problem, software or hardware, can be fixed by solving enough small problems.

  1. De-smokifier

We added an inline FET to our battery power rail in case people put the batteries in the wrong way. The problem was realizing this so late. We didn’t have another PVT to test our change, so we had to make the change and hope for the best. I did do a rework with a FET I had lying around to make sure that it passed some for of smoke check. We also hedged our bets by adding a bypass jumper in case this fix broke something.

(Yes my rework abilities could use some work. In fairness to me, I was soldering to a ground fill.)

This did work!

  1. Orientation dots

Normally PCB silkscreens include small dots to help orient components. Unfortunately, we didn’t incorporate those into our design. We reversed this decision and just included the dots. They weren’t noticeable and helped a ton in our final run to double check orientations.

  1. Clarity in emails

We realized a large portion of our failures were in communication. Having detailed and …

I wrote JLC a long email about the PVT. What made it work wasn’t the tone, it was the evidence: I attached five photos, named good_1, good_2, bad_1, bad_2, bad_3 — the correctly bonded screen from the previous order alongside what I’d just received, so there was no ambiguity about what “attached” meant.

And the most useful thing in it wasn’t a complaint at all. It was this question:

“What is the best way for us to document this requirement so it works well for your process — a dedicated assembly drawing, a process note on the order, attached reference photos, or referencing the previous order number? We’re happy to provide whatever makes it unambiguous on the production floor.”

Ask the factory how to tell the factory things. They know. Nobody does this, so they’re pleased when you do.

Preparing the badges

Our process was simple but time consuming:

  1. Open the box and remove the bubble wrap
  2. Take the badges out, and stack them with one foam in between each
  3. Snap the rail (the other side’s rail was snapped by JLC for access to the USB-C port)
  4. Clean off the sides with steel wool (we switched to using the rails themselves after)
  5. Flash it (this was so fun we decided to do it twice!)
  6. Attach the event lanyard
  7. Re-package

We started at 7pm. We finished at 3am. It takes a truly devoted team to pull this off. Thank you team!

Closing

This is for hackathon organizers:

When we started, hackathon hardware badges were rare. We wanted to know why, gave it our best, and managed to pull it off. We did it with a few people, a credit card, and a little grit. The factory is ultimately a web form, the parts are just rows in a table, and mistakes are expensive but survivable.

So here’s my challenge to you: beat us.

Make the color silkscreen we couldn’t. Build a better power architecture. Integrate the badge better into your hackathon. Put a better radio, maybe even connect to event Wi-Fi. Get a nicer screen. Use a more powerful MCU. Spec it to run Linux. Add a crypt chip. Make it bend or fold.

This post exists to hand you receipts proving it’s possible and a not-so-crazy idea.

I can’t wait to see what you do.

P.P.S.

About my friend at Hack the North, Alex. Put plainly, without Alex you wouldn’t be reading this. I can’t even put in to words how much he helped me this term with the project.

He was assigned as my PM from the very beginning. My reaction was “great, they’re giving me a baby sitter, but I guess that’s fair given how much money is going to be thrown at this”. Boy was I wrong.

Completing projects has always been incredibly hard for me. If you run a quick ls -l | wc -l in my programming directory, you get roughly 380. And no, that’s not the directory I use to clone things. Those are all my projects from 12 years of being a developer. One of those is finished. Hacker Badges.

Don’t get me wrong, all of those were incredibly valuable for their educational value, and they allowed me to gain a very broad set of skills, but it would have been nice to ship one.

When I told my parents after the event, I think they were about as surprised as I was that I actually finished what I set out to do. What was missing with all the other projects? Alex.

Getting a project like this off the ground put my exact weaknesses on full display, and Alex was able to immediately jump in and keep me going.

He also has a great quote that is going to stick with me, and taint all future PMs I have. “A good PM will see a problem and not go find who to complain to, but just fix it themselves if they can”. This guy jumped right into the firmware directory with all the backlogged polish items. He even ended up with more commits than me.

Beyond just this project, he managed backend, frontend, and had a hand in literally every other subteam. He truly put in 1000 hours since February to make the hacker experience magical. This meant grinding through 3 hours of calls EVERY SINGLE DAY after his long day at his software internship. He could have spent that time grinding the internship, but instead had a vision for Hack the North that he could not let go unfulfilled. There are very few dedicated to anything, let alone hackathons, as Alex is to Hack the North. There was even a time he was interviewing for an incredible opportunity in software engineering, it’s probably the one you’re thinking of, and he was texting me with some QA he was doing on the badge during the short lunch break between his interviews. (He got the job btw, obviously deserved.) By the end of the term, I learned that playing on anyone else’s team is playing for second place. People like this are so rare they are almost singular in most of our careers.

Good luck Alex!
